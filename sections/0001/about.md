In 1860, Giuseppe Garibaldi led the Redshirts from Sicily to the Italian mainland. His daring march over the Strait of Messina and the subsequent occupation of Naples dismantled Bourbon rule and created a political vacuum that Italian nationalists could fill. The swift capture of the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies demonstrated how a charismatic figure could translate popular fervor into territorial gains.

Count Camillo di Cavour, serving as Prime Minister of Piedmont, negotiated the 1861 Congress of Berlin to secure recognition of Italian independence. Cavour’s diplomatic acumen ensured that the newly proclaimed Kingdom of Italy received support from Britain and France, allowing the Piedmontese crown to annex Lombardy–Venetia. The 1861 consolidation of northern and central Italian states laid the groundwork for a unified political structure.

Cavour’s secret alliance with Napoleon III in 1866 secured French support for the Austro‑Italian war. The 1866 conflict forced Austria to relinquish control of the Lombardy region, while the subsequent Franco‑Italian Treaty of 1867 granted Italy the Duchy of Trieste. These maneuvers illustrated how diplomatic calculations could compensate for military setbacks, expanding Italy’s borders through negotiated settlements.

While Prussian unification depended on Bismarck's Realpolitik, Italy achieved unity through Garibaldi's charismatic campaigns and Cavour's diplomatic strategies.
